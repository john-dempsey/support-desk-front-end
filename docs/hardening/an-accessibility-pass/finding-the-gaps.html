Finding the Gaps

Three ways to check an app's accessibility: the keyboard, Lighthouse and a screen reader. Together they found three gaps in the Support Desk app.

Stage 0 built the app to be accessible from the start, and every stage since has kept to it. An accessibility pass checks that it worked: you use the app the way people with different needs use it, and note anything that gets in their way.

Three ways to look

  1. The keyboard only. Put the mouse away and use Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter and Space. Can you reach every link, button and field? Can you always see where you are? Is the order sensible? Plenty of people never use a mouse: people who can't, and people who find the keyboard faster.
  2. Lighthouse, built into Chrome's DevTools. It runs dozens of automatic checks on a page and gives it a score. It's quick, and it needs nothing installed.
  3. A screen reader for a few minutes: NVDA (free) on Windows, or VoiceOver (built in) on a Mac. Close your eyes, or turn the screen off, and try to find a ticket. A few minutes of this teaches more than any checklist.

The numbers are WCAG's success criteria (Stage 0's "Colour, Contrast and the Keyboard" introduced WCAG). The rest of this topic fixes each gap in turn.

One more gap was found and left as a challenge: after clicking a link, keyboard focus stays where it was, so a screen reader doesn't announce the new page. Fixing it touches every page, and it's in this lesson's "Try it yourself".

Try it

Do the first check on your own app: keyboard only, for five minutes. Log in, open something, change something, and log out, without touching the mouse. Write down anything that was slow, confusing or impossible. Keep the list: the next slides may fix some of it.
