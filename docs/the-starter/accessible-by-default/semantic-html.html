Semantic HTML

Using the HTML element that matches the meaning, so screen readers and keyboards can find their way around.

Accessibility means people can use your app whatever their abilities are: someone who is blind and uses a screen reader (software that reads the page aloud), someone who can't use a mouse and moves around with the keyboard, someone with low vision who zooms in. It isn't optional extra polish. In Ireland, public-sector websites are legally required to meet accessibility standards, and since June 2025 the European Accessibility Act applies similar rules to many private services, such as online shops and banking.

The standard everyone measures against is WCAG (the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines). Level AA is the usual target, and it's the one this module aims for.

The cheapest and most effective accessibility work is choosing the right HTML element for each job. That's called semantic HTML: the element says what the thing is, not just how it looks.

What the starter uses

Element Where What it gives a screen reader user
<html lang="en"> index.html the right pronunciation: the reader uses an English voice
<header> NavBar a "banner" landmark at the top of the page
<nav aria-label="Main"> NavBar a "Main navigation" landmark they can jump straight to
<ul> of links NavBar "list, 1 item", so they know how many links there are
<main> App a "main" landmark, to skip past the navigation to the content
one <h1> per page every page the page's title; screen reader users often jump between headings
<Link> (a real <a>) NotFoundPage, NavBar announced as a link, reachable with Tab, opened with Enter

Landmarks (header, nav, main) are like a table of contents for the page. Screen readers have a key to jump between them, the way you'd glance at the top of a page to find the menu.

aria-label="Main" gives the <nav> a name. It matters as soon as a page has more than one <nav>, such as a footer menu, so users can tell them apart.

What not to do

<div className="nav">
  <div onClick={goHome}>Home</div>
</div>

This can be styled to look identical to the real thing, but a screen reader just hears "Home" with no hint that it's clickable. The keyboard can't reach it with Tab, and Enter does nothing. A real <a> or <button> gets all of that from the browser for free.

The rule: use a <button> for actions and a link for navigation, and reach for a <div> only when nothing more meaningful fits.
